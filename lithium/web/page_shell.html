Lithos
A design for lithium care · research prototype

Make every lithium level count

Much of what makes clinicians wary of lithium is the work of managing it: blood tests timed to the hour, interacting drugs prescribed by someone else, kidneys that need watching for decades. Lithos is a design for a lithium care system that takes that work off people's hands, with a Bayesian engine at its core. Whether that leads to more appropriate prescribing is the hypothesis it is built to test.

The slip shows a real engine calculation. The blood was drawn at 3:30 in the afternoon, 18.5 hours after the evening dose; the engine reports what a correctly timed 12-hour level would have shown.

A level drawn whenever suits the patient

Pick a situation or change the numbers. The engine fits two published-model priors to the patient's kidney function, size and dosing history, weighs them by how well each explains the level, and reports the standardised 12-hour level with a 90% credible interval, the dose most likely to land in the target range, and when to check again. The patients are examples; nothing here is for clinical use.

    The design

    Five things it does that nothing else does together

    Every blood test counts, whenever it is taken

    Any sample with a recorded dose time is converted to the standardised 12-hour level every guideline is written in, with an honest interval, including before steady state. It builds on Amdisen's 1977 standard and the recent eLi12 work, and adds individual models, uncertainty and history. When a sample can't support a decision, it says so and asks for a better-timed one.

    About half of real-world levels are not drawn at 12 hours.

    The right dose in fewer tests

    The first dose comes from kidney function, age and body composition; every level updates a personal model, and dose changes don't have to wait a week for steady state.

    Published single models predict poorly on new patients, so the engine weighs several.

    Forecasts, not alerts

    When a GP selects ramipril or a thiazide, the software forecasts this patient's new level, proposes the compensating dose and books the check level: in the prescribing screen where the software allows it, and through the pharmacy and lithium team where it doesn't. The same logic covers sick days, heatwaves, surgery and childbirth.

    Starting an interacting drug: ~30-fold odds of toxicity, even with alerts firing.

    Monitoring follows risk

    The guideline calendar is the floor. The forecast brings tests forward for people near the ceiling, on interacting drugs or with falling eGFR, and the kidney trajectory is tracked for life with NICE and KDIGO rules.

    Fully compliant monitoring reaches only about one patient in six.

    Nobody has to remember

    Recall, pathology requests, reminders, results, interpretation, sign-off and patient instructions form one loop shared by the patient, GP, psychiatrist, lab and pharmacist.

    Registers with active recall work; reminders alone did not.

    In-silico trials

    What the simulations show

    Simulations test whether the mechanisms work under stated assumptions; they are not evidence of clinical benefit. The virtual patients come from a deliberately different model than the engine uses, miss doses and are sampled at realistic times.

    Why this hasn't happened yet

    The gap is a system, not an algorithm

    Bayesian lithium forecasting has been published since the late 1980s, and none of the established precision-dosing products lists lithium. Three drugs and one lab calculation show what it takes.

    Getting it used

    Start where adoption is frictionless

    Lithium is a cheap generic with no commercial sponsor, so the plan leans on pathology labs, specialist clinics and an open, public-good engine.

    0–6 months

    Validate

    Validate on real patients with recorded dose times against a pre-specified plan: MIMIC-IV hospital records (the converter is ready), then the eLi12 group and lithium clinics. Check the rules against RANZCP 2020. Run a clinician workspace in one lithium clinic, with a go/no-go on capturing dose times for at least 80% of levels.

    6–18 months

    Shadow and pilot

    Run silently alongside care; pilot a standardised 12-hour level on a pathology partner's reports; add interaction forecasts in one GP software system and a perinatal module.

    18–36 months

    Trial and standardise

    A stepped-wedge trial with lithium time-in-range as the primary outcome; the patient app; point-of-care testing for rural and remote sites; and standards work on reporting and the metric.

    Research prototype. Not a medical device and not for clinical use. Model parameters are provisional: one prior is assembled from published physiology and the other's variability terms are assumed. Both agree with published cohorts at the population level but have not been validated on individual patients. Simulated results test mechanisms, not clinical benefit. Guideline rules follow NICE CG185 and ISBD/IGSLi 2019; RANZCP 2020 could not be checked.
